Road and Fire Safety Rules Every Young Child Should Know

By Sathish Kumar · November 21, 2026 · For ages 4-10 · 4 min read

Young children are not small adults when it comes to danger. They find it hard to judge how fast a car is moving, they are easily distracted, and they often freeze or hide when frightened. Rules help, but only when they are short, repeated often and practised in real places. Here are the rules we think matter most, and how to teach them.

Road safety

Stop, look, listen, think

Teach your child to stop at the edge of the pavement, look all around for traffic, listen for engines, and only cross when the road is clear. Say it out loud together every time you cross, even when you are in a hurry. Children copy what we do far more than what we say.

Hold hands near roads

Most young children cannot cross roads safely on their own. Many road safety organisations suggest that children under about 8 should cross with an adult, because they cannot yet judge the speed and distance of traffic reliably. Holding hands, or holding the pram or bag, is a simple rule with no exceptions.

Use crossings and never cross between parked cars

Drivers cannot see a small child stepping out from between parked cars, and the child cannot see the car. Use zebra crossings, traffic lights or a clear, straight stretch of road.

Balls and pets do not count

If a ball rolls into the road, the rule is "stop and get a grown-up". Practise saying it at home, because the urge to chase is very strong in the moment.

Be seen

Bright or reflective clothing helps drivers spot children in rain and at dusk. A helmet is essential on a bicycle or scooter.

Fire safety

Get out, stay out

If there is a fire, the only job is to get out quickly and never go back inside for toys, pets or belongings. Many children believe they should hide under a bed or in a cupboard. Explain kindly that hiding makes it harder for firefighters to find them.

Crawl low under smoke

Smoke rises, so the cleanest air is near the floor. Practise crawling on hands and knees across a room as a game.

Check doors before opening

Older children can learn to touch a closed door with the back of their hand. If it is hot, use another way out.

Stop, drop and roll

If clothes catch fire, running makes the flames worse. Stop, drop to the ground, cover your face and roll until the fire is out. Children enjoy practising this, which is exactly why it sticks.

Matches and lighters are tools for adults

Teach children to tell an adult if they find matches or a lighter, rather than touching them.

Make a family escape plan

Draw a simple map of your home together. Mark two ways out of every room and choose a meeting point outside, such as a particular tree or a neighbour's gate. Practise the plan twice a year, once in daylight and once in the evening. Test your smoke alarms monthly and let your child press the test button so the sound is familiar, not frightening.

Emergency numbers

Children should know how to call for help and what to say: their name, their address and what is happening. The number depends on where you live, for example 112 in India and across the European Union, 911 in the United States and Canada, and 999 in the United Kingdom. Write your local number and your address on a card near the phone, and practise a pretend call with a toy phone. Explain that emergency numbers are only for real emergencies.

How to teach without scaring

  • Keep the tone calm and matter-of-fact. The message is "we know what to do", not "something bad will happen".
  • Practise rules as games and role-play, then in real places.
  • Praise your child for remembering a rule, especially when they remind you.
  • Ask "what would you do if...?" questions on walks and car journeys.
