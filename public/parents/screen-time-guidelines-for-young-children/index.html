Screen Time for Young Children: What the Guidelines Say and How to Make It Count

By Sathish Kumar · November 21, 2026 · For ages 2-12 · 3 min read

We make games, so it may seem odd for us to write about limiting screen time. But we believe a short, well-chosen session with a parent nearby is worth far more than hours of passive scrolling, and that families deserve clear, honest information. Here is what the major health guidelines say, and how we try to follow them in our own design.

What the guidelines say

The World Health Organization published guidance in 2019 on physical activity, sleep and sedentary behaviour for children under 5. It recommends no sedentary screen time for babies under 1 year or for 1-year-olds. For children aged 2 to 4, it recommends no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time a day, and says less is better.

The American Academy of Pediatrics gives similar advice. It suggests avoiding screen media other than video chatting for children younger than 18 months. For 18 to 24 months, it suggests choosing high-quality content and watching it with your child. For children aged 2 to 5, it recommends limiting use to about 1 hour a day of high-quality programming, ideally watched or played together.

For school-age children, the guidance focuses less on a fixed number of minutes and more on balance. Screens should not replace sleep, physical activity, meals together, homework or face-to-face play. A family media plan, agreed together, helps.

Not all screen time is the same

Researchers often distinguish between passive use, such as watching video after video, and active use, where a child is thinking, choosing and solving problems. They also note that co-use, where a parent plays or watches alongside and talks about it, helps children learn more from what they see. A child sorting colours in a puzzle while you ask "why did you move that one?" is doing something quite different from a child alone with autoplay.

Practical tips for families

  • Set a clear end before you start. "Three levels, then shoes on" works better than an open-ended session. Short levels make this easier.
  • Play together when you can. Ask your child to teach you the game. Explaining is one of the best ways to learn.
  • Connect the screen to the real world. After a clock game, read the kitchen clock. After a science puzzle, do the experiment.
  • Keep screens out of bedtime. Many paediatric guidelines suggest turning devices off at least an hour before sleep and keeping them out of bedrooms overnight.
  • Check what a game asks for. Be cautious with games that need accounts, have chat with strangers, push in-app purchases or use loot boxes.
  • Watch for warning signs. Tantrums when a session ends, lost sleep or giving up other activities are signals to cut back.

How we try to design responsibly

  • Our learning games use short levels, so there is a natural place to stop.
  • There are no accounts, chat or in-game purchases. Progress is saved only in your own browser.
  • We pay for the site with advertising. Ads are requested with Google's child-directed settings, so they are not personalised, and they appear only between levels, never in the middle of play.
  • Each game page has ideas for continuing the learning away from the screen.
  • Our action games are labelled with age guidance and are kept separate from the learning games.

The bottom line

For the youngest children, less is better and together is best. For older children, aim for balance and choose content that makes them think. A few minutes of a good game, followed by a real conversation, can be a genuinely useful part of a child's day.

This article summarises public guidance for general information. It is not medical advice. If you are worried about your child's development or screen use, talk to your doctor or health visitor.
