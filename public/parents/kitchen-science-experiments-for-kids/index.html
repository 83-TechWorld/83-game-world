5 Safe Kitchen Science Experiments for Kids (with the Science Explained)

By Sathish Kumar · November 21, 2026 · For ages 5-12 · 5 min read

You do not need a lab to do real science with children. Most of these experiments use things already in the kitchen, take less than half an hour and lead to good questions. Each one comes with a short explanation you can give at a child's level, and a "what if?" to try next.

Before you start: safety

  • An adult should be in charge of every experiment, especially anything hot.
  • Nothing in an experiment is for eating or drinking, even if it started as food.
  • Protect eyes from splashes. Swimming goggles or sunglasses work well.
  • Never put a lid on a fizzing mixture. The gas needs somewhere to go.
  • Wash hands and wipe surfaces afterwards.

1. Red cabbage colour indicator

You need: a few red cabbage leaves, hot water from the kettle (adult job), a jug, clear cups, lemon juice, vinegar, baking soda and soapy water.

What to do: Tear the leaves into the jug, cover with hot water and leave for 15 minutes. Strain off the purple liquid and let it cool. Pour a little into each cup and add a different kitchen liquid to each one.

What happens: Lemon juice and vinegar turn it pink or red. Baking soda turns it blue or green. Plain water leaves it purple.

The science: Red cabbage contains natural colours called anthocyanins that change shape, and so change colour, depending on whether a liquid is an acid or a base. Scientists measure this on the pH scale. Acids have a low pH and bases have a high pH. Your cabbage juice is a homemade pH indicator.

What if? Ask your child to predict a colour before testing something new, like milk or toothpaste mixed with water.

2. Baking soda volcano

You need: a tray, a small bottle or cup, two spoons of baking soda, a squeeze of washing-up liquid, food colouring and vinegar.

What to do: Put the bottle on the tray, add the baking soda, soap and colouring, then pour in vinegar.

The science: Vinegar (an acid) reacts with baking soda (a base). The reaction makes a new substance: carbon dioxide gas. The soap traps the gas in bubbles, so the foam rises and spills over. This is a chemical change, because new substances are made and you cannot easily turn them back.

What if? Does warm vinegar fizz faster than cold vinegar? Does more baking soda make a bigger eruption, or does it stop when the vinegar runs out?

3. The floating egg

You need: a raw egg, a tall glass of water and lots of salt.

What to do: Lower the egg into plain water. It sinks. Take it out, stir in salt a spoonful at a time and try again.

The science: Whether something floats depends on its density, which is how much stuff is packed into its size. An egg is a little denser than fresh water, so it sinks. Dissolving salt makes the water denser. When the water becomes denser than the egg, the egg floats. This is also why people float more easily in the sea than in a swimming pool.

What if? Gently pour fresh water on top of very salty water. Can you make the egg hover in the middle?

4. Ruler see-saw (levers)

You need: a 30 cm ruler, a pencil and some coins of the same size.

What to do: Balance the ruler on the pencil at its middle. Put two coins near one end. Where must one coin go on the other side to balance them?

The science: The pencil is the pivot, or fulcrum. A weight far from the pivot has more turning power than the same weight close to it. Two coins 5 cm from the middle balance one coin 10 cm from the middle. This is why a small child can lift a grown-up on a see-saw by sitting at the very end, and why a long spanner makes it easier to undo a bolt.

What if? Move the pencil off-centre. Can you balance one coin against three?

5. Growing salt or sugar crystals

You need: hot water (adult job), salt or sugar, a clean jar, a pencil and a piece of string.

What to do: Stir salt into the hot water until no more will dissolve. Pour it into the jar, tie the string to the pencil and rest the pencil across the top so the string hangs in the liquid. Leave the jar somewhere still for a few days.

The science: Hot water can hold more dissolved salt than cold water. As the water cools and slowly evaporates, there is not enough water to hold all the salt, so it comes out of the solution and builds up as crystals with flat faces and straight edges. This is a physical change: the salt is still salt.

Talking about it afterwards

The talk after an experiment is where most of the learning happens. Three questions work for any age: "What did you think would happen?", "What actually happened?" and "Why do you think it was different?" Writing or drawing the results in a small notebook turns play into a habit of thinking like a scientist.
