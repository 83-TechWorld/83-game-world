How to Teach a Child to Tell the Time on a Clock with Hands

By Sathish Kumar · November 21, 2026 · For ages 5-9 · 4 min read

Many children can read "3:30" on a phone long before they can read the same time on a clock with hands. That is normal. A digital clock only asks a child to read numbers. A clock with hands asks them to understand two moving pointers, two different scales on one face and the idea that time keeps going round. It is a big idea, so it is best taught in small steps over several months.

When are children ready?

Children differ a lot, but a common order is the one used in many school curricula. Around ages 5 to 6, children learn o'clock and half past. Around 6 to 7, they move on to quarter past, quarter to and counting in fives. By about 7 to 8, most can read the time to the exact minute. If your child can count to 60 and count in fives, they have the number skills they need. If not, practise those first.

Step 1: Only the hour hand

Start with a clock that shows just one hand, or cover the long hand with your finger. Point out that the short hand is the hour hand. When it points straight at a number, that number is the hour. Ask "What number is the short hand pointing at?" many times a day, at real moments: lunch at 12, bath at 6, bed at 7.

Next, show what happens between numbers. When the hour hand is halfway between 3 and 4, it is "after 3 but not yet 4". This one idea prevents the most common mistake later, which is reading half past 3 as half past 4 because the hand is close to the 4.

Step 2: O'clock and half past

Now add the long minute hand. When it points straight up at 12, we say "o'clock". When it points straight down at 6, it has gone halfway round, so we say "half past". A paper plate cut in half, or a pizza with half eaten, makes "half past" easy to picture.

Keep the hour rule from step 1. At half past 3, the hour hand is halfway between 3 and 4. The hour is still 3.

Step 3: Quarter past and quarter to

Cut the plate into four. When the minute hand has gone a quarter of the way round, pointing at 3, it is "quarter past". When it has gone three quarters of the way, pointing at 9, there is only a quarter left before the next hour, so it is "quarter to". Quarter to is the trickiest phrase for children, because the hour named is the next hour. Quarter to 5 means the hour hand is nearly at 5, not just past it.

Step 4: Counting in fives

The numbers on the clock face are hour numbers, but each one also stands for five minutes. Walk your finger round the face counting "5, 10, 15, 20..." up to 60. Children enjoy writing the five-minute numbers on small sticky notes around a real clock. Once they can count round, times like "25 past 7" and "20 to 9" become counting puzzles rather than things to memorise.

Step 5: Exact minutes

Finally, show the small marks between the numbers. Each mark is one minute. To read 7:23, count in fives to 20, then count on in ones: 21, 22, 23. At this stage, connect the clock to digital time. Write 7:23 next to the clock and show that both say the same thing.

Everyday ways to practise

  • Use a real clock with hands at home. A cheap wall clock at a child's eye level helps more than any worksheet.
  • Tie times to routines. "When the long hand is on the 6, we turn the TV off." Children learn fastest when the time matters to them.
  • Let them be the clock. Children stand with one arm short and one arm long and make the time you call out.
  • Ask "how long until...?" Counting forward ("from 4 o'clock to half past 4 is 30 minutes") builds time sense, not just reading.
  • Keep it short. Five minutes a day works better than a long session at the weekend.

Common mistakes to look out for

  • Mixing up the hour and minute hands. Remind them that the short word, "hour", goes with the short hand.
  • Reading the hour from the closest number instead of the last number passed.
  • Saying "quarter to 4" when they mean quarter past 4. Ask "Is it nearly 4, or just after 4?"
  • Thinking the minute hand pointing at 2 means 2 minutes. Go back to counting in fives.
